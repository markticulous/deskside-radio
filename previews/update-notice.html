Where an update should announce itself

The check already runs and already knows. What it has never had is anywhere to say so outside the Settings drawer. Five placements below, each drawn into the radio’s own top bar across four of the eight themes — whatever is chosen has to sit in one place and read the same way in all of them.

The dismiss, as built

B was chosen and shipped. What it did not have was a way to stop saying it. The pill now carries a ring-and-cross at its right-hand end — every pill below is live: press one and watch it go. “Restore” at the foot stands in for the next completed check.

• The dot blinks The pill is small, quiet and off to one side of the plate, which is what it should be — and which is also how it gets missed. The dot is the one thing on the face that moves of its own accord, so it is what catches the eye that was not looking. Two seconds a cycle, lit for three quarters of it: a marker with a pulse in it rather than an alarm. It snaps on and fades off over 60ms — the snap is what the eye catches, and a dot that eased in as well would read as something still loading. The pill itself fades in over 220ms when it arrives — the one movement here meant to be watched rather than caught — and goes out over 90ms on the cross. Going is quicker than arriving on purpose: an arrival is news and a dismissal is not, and a control that vanishes in the frame your finger lands in reads as a misclick rather than as a thing done. It is the same orange whatever theme you are in, and deliberately not the theme’s own colour: cut from each cabinet’s byline it was brass on the dial and lime on the board, one piece of news wearing eight faces, each in a colour that theme already uses for something ordinary.

  • Pressing it hides the pill and remembers that, so it stays hidden across a restart.
  • The next check that comes back — the six-hourly one, or Check now — clears it, and the pill returns if the update is still outstanding.
  • The dot on the Settings control is never hidden by it. That one is a fact about the machine; the pill is an announcement about it.
  • Both disappear for good the moment the running version catches up with the published one.

What the notice opens

The pill was a link to the releases page, which is the one thing the reader could have found on their own. It now opens a dialog — on Windows, where a launcher is actually fetching things; elsewhere it stays the link it was. Which of the two states appears is decided at the moment of the click, by reading what is on disk right then: the launcher updates the folder while the radio plays, so the files can already be a version ahead of the window being looked at.

After an update, the readout says so

An update replaces the files under a closed radio, and by the time anybody sees anything the installer’s window has gone. The version in Settings is a place nobody looks on the way past. So the radio says it once, on the part of the front that is always being read — five flashes, then it goes back to work. Press play below to watch it.

Four blinks at a second each. It began at 360ms and then 560, and both read as a fault rather than as an announcement — fast blinking is what a thing does when it has gone wrong. The fifth blink goes out exactly like the four before it and stays out; what follows is a pause, and the pause is what makes the ending an ending. Set in capitals, like every other alert on the front. Three cabinets are shown because two of them do not set the readout as text: the console builds one segment cell per character and the departures board one flap per character. Both were checked in the app and take the announcement whole — eighteen cells and eighteen flaps, nothing clipped, nothing set scrolling. Neither is the real thing here: the board turns simply drawn flaps rather than the app’s folded half-flaps, and the console is a dot field and grid wires behind a monospace face rather than the app’s 14-segment one. What is being shown is the timing. The board flaps the announcement in once and flaps back to the station at the end; the blinking in between is the whole board going dark and light, not the flaps turning six times. Then the station comes back over 220ms, the same fade the update pill arrives with — the two are the only things on the face that announce themselves, and they should move alike. It is said once and never again: the profile records which version last ran in it, and only a change to that number brings this up. A first install says nothing at all, because nothing was updated.

What I would pick

A, on its own

A dot on Settings is the only treatment that costs the radio nothing. It adds no object to a bar that eight themes each lay out differently, needs no colour of its own beyond the theme accent already in use, and points at the drawer where the version line and the releases link already live.

An update is not urgent. It is worth knowing about and worth ignoring for a fortnight, which is exactly what a dot says and a pill does not.

recommended

B, if it must be readable

Pick this if the dot would go unnoticed on your machine for months. It is the only option that names the thing, and the wordmark end of the bar has room in every theme.

Cost: a new object in the header that each theme has to be checked against, and one more thing competing with the schedule chip for attention.

Not D or E

The hairline collides with the countdown that already runs along the schedule chip in the last minute of a slot — two hairlines meaning different things is worse than no hairline.

The toast covers the station name, which is the one thing the radio exists to show.

The Service line, reworded

What Check now reports afterwards, in the three states it can land in. The middle one stopped naming a download the day the launcher began fetching by itself: the copy already in the app folder is both quicker and free of the two security prompts a fresh download costs, and it is named in the pane above this line rather than in the line itself.

How often

Every six hours

As asked. Four requests a day to one static file, still nothing sent but the request. A radio left open all week would ask about twenty-eight times instead of seven.

It only helps where the app stays open for days at a time — which is exactly what this app is for, so it is the right call.

What it changes

One constant: CHECK_EVERY from 24 hours to 6. The stored timestamp and the forced check behind Check now are untouched.

The readmes both say “once a day” in two places and would be corrected with it.
