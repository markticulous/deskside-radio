Tooltips

Five candidates, each shown on four of the eight faces. Hover the icons and the chip in any cabinet — every tooltip here is live and positioned the way the real one would be.

The first four read the same three values — background, ink and line — which each theme names for itself. That is the same arrangement the byline already uses, and it is what lets one rule serve eight faces.

Two things to watch for, not just the look. Each cabinet below clips its contents, exactly as the real one does — so notice how a tooltip on the top row has to escape upward past the cabinet edge. And the retro face has no anti-aliasing to hide behind: a rounded corner or a soft shadow reads as wrong there in a way it does not elsewhere.
A

Plain plate

Background, ink, a soft shadow, nothing else. Quietest and least likely to fight any face — and the least like a radio.

B

Bordered plate

The same, with a hairline in the theme's own line colour. Reads as a piece of the cabinet rather than something floating over it.

C

With a pointer

Bordered, plus a small arrow at the control it belongs to. Unambiguous when several controls sit close together — which is exactly the top bar.

D

In the face's own voice

Hard edge, offset shadow, tracked caps, and the cabinet's own typeface. Most characterful; also the most work, and the easiest to get wrong on a face it was not drawn for.

E

The app’s own chrome

Identical on all eight faces — the same white plate, hairline and radius the Settings drawer, the confirm dialogs and the finder list already wear, now with a pointer joined to the plate. Note it is the only option here that does not change between cabinets.

Why E is on the list. The sheet already states this policy, about Settings: “Settings wears one neutral light chrome whatever the tuner is wearing… re-skinning it per theme meant reading forms through a pixel face or a gold serif on near-black for no gain.” A tooltip is chrome by that definition, so E is what the codebase already argues for — and A–D are all departures from it. That may still be the right departure: a tooltip is a glance, not a form to read.
